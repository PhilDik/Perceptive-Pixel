# Perceptive Pixel

## September 2026 Extension of the Screen Scanner Concept

This document describes a later hardware extension of the **Screen Scanner** concept originally published in this repository in June 2026.

The June 2026 concept already proposed a reversible display surface capable of alternating between light emission and optical sensing.

The original concept also described:

- controlled illumination generated by the display itself;
- temporal modulation and blinking patterns;
- multiple colors or frequencies;
- measurement of reflected optical responses;
- multiple observations over time;
- movement of the device;
- computational reconstruction of depth and volume;
- possible neural-network reconstruction trained against real depth measurements.

The September 2026 extension does not replace these principles.

Instead, it changes the proposed physical sensing architecture of the individual display pixel.

---

## 1. From a Single Optical Measurement to a Perceptive Pixel

A conventional reversible display element can be represented approximately as:

**Pixel(x,y) → Emit / Detect(L)**

During display operation, the element emits light.

During sensing, it provides an optical measurement of the light reaching that element.

The proposed **Perceptive Pixel** extends the sensing state by dividing the optical response of a physical display pixel into multiple independently readable regions or facets.

For an element with **n** sensing regions:

**P(x,y,t) = (L1, L2, ... Ln)**

Each value represents a real optical measurement obtained from a different physical region or orientation of the same display pixel.

These measurements are not calculated depth coordinates.

They are not a finished image.

They are separate samples of the local optical field reaching the pixel.

Depth and geometry are reconstructed later by combining measurements across the display and across time.

---

## 2. Multi-Facet Optical Sampling

Different facets or sensing regions may receive different amounts and distributions of incident, reflected, scattered or refracted light.

Consequently, a single physical display coordinate can provide several optical observations instead of one scalar intensity measurement.

The objective is to preserve more spatial information about the incoming optical field before computational reconstruction.

The number of sensing regions is not fixed.

Possible configurations include:

- single-region reversible pixels;
- three-facet pixels;
- four-facet pixels;
- higher-order or asymmetric structures;
- curved or microstructured sensing surfaces.

The optimal configuration must be determined experimentally and computationally.

---

## 3. Tri-Facet Perceptive Pixel

A particularly interesting candidate is a **three-facet Perceptive Pixel**:

**P(x,y,t) = (L1, L2, L3)**

The three sensing regions have different physical orientations and independently measure the light reaching them.

A three-facet structure may provide a useful compromise between:

- spatial diversity of optical measurements;
- available photosensitive area;
- electrical complexity;
- readout bandwidth;
- computational cost;
- optical isolation;
- inter-pixel crosstalk.

Three sensing regions require less readout and processing than four or more regions while potentially retaining enough measurement diversity for spatial reconstruction.

Three facets are therefore proposed as a candidate architecture, not as a demonstrated optimum.

Adjacent rows may be shifted, mirrored or rotated to create a dense repeating matrix.

This arrangement could allow neighboring sensing surfaces to face different directions rather than exposing identical sensing orientations to adjacent emitters.

The matrix geometry could therefore be optimized simultaneously for:

1. obtaining spatially diverse measurements of external light;
2. reducing direct optical coupling between neighboring display elements.

If direct light from neighboring pixels becomes a major source of interference, alternating triangular orientations may reduce some direct emitter-to-detector paths.

This is a hypothesis to be tested through optical simulation and physical prototypes.

---

## 5. Display and Sensing Operation

During normal display operation, the regions of a Perceptive Pixel may operate together as one visible emitting element.

During sensing intervals, the regions are read independently.

The same physical element therefore has two principal states:

**DISPLAY MODE**

multiple regions → combined light emission

**SENSING MODE**

multiple regions → independent optical measurements

The exact semiconductor structure, contacts, readout circuitry, facet geometry and optical isolation are not yet specified.

A practical implementation may require a purpose-built optoelectronic pixel structure.

---

## 6. Perceptive Display Matrix

A display constructed from these elements produces a multidimensional measurement field:

**M(x,y,k,t)**

where:

- **x,y** identify the position of the pixel in the display;
- **k** identifies the sensing region or facet;
- **t** identifies measurement time and therefore potentially a different display pose.

Instead of obtaining only:

**I(x,y,t)**

the system obtains multiple optical observations at each display coordinate.

For a three-facet implementation:

**M(x,y,t) = (L1, L2, L3)**

across the entire display surface.

---

## 7. Motion-Assisted Reconstruction

The Perceptive Pixel does not independently calculate depth.

Spatial reconstruction is performed using the complete moving matrix.

At one display position the system records:

**M(x,y,k,t1)**

After movement:

**M(x,y,k,t2)**

Further movement produces additional observations.

The reconstruction algorithm can compare changes:

- between facets of the same pixel;
- between neighboring pixels;
- across the complete display;
- between successive device positions;
- across different illumination states.

The proposed information pipeline is:

**multi-facet optical sampling  
→ movement of the display  
→ changing optical measurements  
→ correlation across facets, pixels and time  
→ spatial reconstruction**

Movement therefore creates additional views of the optical field.

With sufficiently known device pose, these observations can be combined to estimate scene structure, surface geometry and depth.

---

## 8. Active Illumination

The original Screen Scanner concept already proposed using the display itself as a controlled light source.

This remains part of the Perceptive Display architecture.

The system can know:

- which pixels emitted light;
- when they emitted;
- their intensity;
- their wavelength or color;
- the spatial illumination pattern.

The display can emit a known pattern and subsequently enter sensing mode.

The returned optical field can then be measured by the multi-facet sensing matrix.

Different illumination patterns, colors, frequencies, intensities and temporal sequences may provide additional constraints for reconstruction.

The same physical surface can therefore potentially perform three roles:

1. **Display**
2. **Active illumination surface**
3. **Multi-facet optical sensing matrix**

---

## 9. Why Multi-Facet Sampling May Help Depth Reconstruction

A single optical measurement is ambiguous.

A change in measured intensity may result from:

- distance;
- surface orientation;
- reflectivity;
- illumination geometry;
- occlusion;
- scattering;
- viewing angle.

Multiple differently oriented measurements provide additional constraints on the same local optical field.

When these measurements are combined with device movement, known display pose and controlled illumination, the inverse reconstruction problem may become better constrained.

The objective of the multi-facet architecture is therefore not to make each pixel directly measure depth.

The objective is to provide the reconstruction system with more independent physical observations.

This may improve reconstruction stability and may increase the useful depth range compared with a single-response sensing matrix.

However, increased depth range has not yet been demonstrated and remains a hypothesis requiring simulation and experiment.

---

## 10. Inter-Pixel Crosstalk

A major engineering challenge is direct optical coupling between neighboring elements.

Light emitted by one display pixel may reach the sensing regions of nearby pixels without interacting with the external scene.

This internal signal could partially obscure the weaker light returning from the environment.

Possible mitigation strategies include:

- alternating facet orientations;
- triangular matrix geometry;
- physical optical barriers;
- directional facet geometry;
- temporal separation of emission and sensing;
- selective illumination patterns;
- differential measurements;
- spectral or polarization separation;
- calibration and subtraction of predictable internal optical coupling.

The proposed tri-facet alternating matrix is partly motivated by this problem.

Its actual effectiveness must be measured rather than assumed.

---

## 11. Computational Complexity

The additional physical information comes at the cost of increased data volume and computation.

A conventional sensing surface may produce approximately:

**I(x,y,t)**

while a Perceptive Display produces:

**M(x,y,k,t)**

Additional dimensions may also represent:

- wavelength;
- illumination pattern;
- exposure state;
- device pose.

Increasing the number of sensing regions therefore increases both the available optical information and the complexity of the reconstruction problem.

The architecture introduces a fundamental trade-off:

**more independent physical observations ↔ greater sensor, readout and computational complexity**

A practical reconstruction system may require:

- calibration of individual facet responses;
- display pose estimation;
- crosstalk compensation;
- temporal correspondence;
- multi-view correlation;
- surface and depth optimization;
- probabilistic reconstruction;
- potentially machine-learning-assisted reconstruction.

This trade-off is another reason to investigate whether three sensing regions may provide sufficient information without the additional complexity of four or more.

---

## 12. Candidate Geometry Optimization

The number of facets should not be selected only by intuition.

Candidate geometries should be compared under identical conditions.

At minimum:

**1 sensing region vs 3 facets vs 4 facets**

should be tested using:

- the same total pixel area;
- the same scene;
- the same illumination power;
- the same display trajectory;
- comparable detector sensitivity;
- identical reconstruction objectives.

Measurements should include:

- reconstruction error;
- depth error;
- optical crosstalk;
- signal-to-noise ratio;
- information gained per additional sensing region;
- readout bandwidth;
- computational cost.

This would determine whether a tri-facet pixel represents a useful balance between measurement diversity and system complexity.

---

## 13. Relationship to Existing Research

Individual components related to this architecture already exist in earlier research and technology, including:

- reversible light-emitting and light-detecting devices;
- displays capable of alternating between emission and sensing;
- angle-sensitive pixels;
- multidirectional photodetectors;
- light-field sensing;
- lensless computational imaging;
- structured illumination;
- multi-view and motion-assisted reconstruction.

The Perceptive Pixel concept does not claim invention of these individual principles.

The proposed extension is the integration of these ideas into a display architecture in which:

**the same physical display pixel can emit light and subsequently provide multiple independently readable optical measurements from differently oriented regions or facets of that pixel.**

A complete matrix of these elements can combine controlled illumination, multi-region optical sensing and device movement for computational reconstruction of the surrounding scene.

A further candidate implementation is a **tri-facet matrix with alternating element orientations intended to reduce inter-pixel optical coupling while retaining spatially diverse sensing.**

An exact prior implementation combining all of these functions in this particular architecture has not yet been identified in the preliminary prior-art search performed during development of this extension.

This should not be interpreted as a definitive claim of novelty.

A broader scientific and patent prior-art review is required.

---

## 14. Current Status and Required Validation

The Perceptive Pixel is currently a proposed architecture rather than a demonstrated device.

Important next steps include:

- optical simulation of different facet geometries;
- comparison of single-, three-, four- and higher-region pixels;
- simulation of triangular and conventional matrix layouts;
- optimization of alternating element orientations;
- modeling of direct neighbor illumination;
- crosstalk analysis;
- detector sensitivity and noise analysis;
- electrical readout design;
- calibration strategy;
- device-motion tracking;
- reconstruction from simulated multi-facet measurements;
- comparison against a conventional single-response sensing matrix;
- estimation of achievable lateral resolution;
- estimation of depth resolution;
- estimation of practical sensing distance.

A particularly important first experiment is a controlled simulation comparing:

**single sensing region per pixel**

**three-facet sensing**

**four-facet sensing**

under identical optical and motion conditions.

The result should determine whether the additional physical measurements improve depth reconstruction sufficiently to justify the increased hardware and computational complexity.

## Concept History

**June 2026 — Screen Scanner**

Initial public concept: reversible display sensing, controlled illumination, temporal modulation, device movement and computational reconstruction of spatial/depth information from a flat optical matrix.

**September 2026 — Perceptive Pixel / Perceptive Display extension**

Multi-region and multi-facet sensing architecture introduced as an extension of the original Screen Scanner concept.

A tri-facet pixel and alternating triangular matrix are proposed as candidate implementations for balancing spatial measurement diversity, inter-pixel optical isolation and computational complexity.

---

## Working Terminology

**Screen Scanner** — original June 2026 concept.

**Perceptive Pixel** — an individual bidirectional multi-region optoelectronic display and sensing element.

**Perceptive Display** — a display matrix composed of Perceptive Pixels and used for active optical sensing and computational spatial reconstruction.

**Tri-Facet Perceptive Pixel** — a candidate three-region implementation intended to combine spatially diverse optical sampling with reduced system complexity and potentially reduced inter-pixel optical crosstalk.
